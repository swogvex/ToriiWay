intro:
  - Hiragana is the first script every learner meets. Each character stands for one sound, usually a consonant followed by a vowel, so か is “ka” and さ is “sa”.
  - Japanese uses it for grammar, such as verb endings and particles like は and を, and for native words that have no common kanji.
  - Books for young children are written almost entirely in hiragana. Once you know it, you can read furigana, the small kana printed above kanji, and sound out any word written in kana.
  - The table has 46 basic characters. Two small marks and a few combinations turn them into every sound of the language.

## Basic 46

The basic table is called <span lang="ja"><ruby>五十音<rt>ごじゅうおん</rt></ruby></span> (gojūon, “fifty sounds”). It is ordered by vowel across, a, i, u, e, o, and by consonant down: k, s, t, n, h, m, y, r, w. Read a row from left to right and you get <span lang="ja">か き く け こ</span> (ka ki ku ke ko).

A few cells break the pattern. <span lang="ja">し</span> is “shi”, <span lang="ja">ち</span> is “chi”, <span lang="ja">つ</span> is “tsu” and <span lang="ja">ふ</span> is “fu”, because that is how Japanese pronounces them. The y-row only has <span lang="ja">や</span>, <span lang="ja">ゆ</span> and <span lang="ja">よ</span>, and the w-row keeps <span lang="ja">わ</span> and <span lang="ja">を</span>. <span lang="ja">ん</span>, the only character that is a consonant on its own, closes the table.

Two characters sound different when they work as particles: <span lang="ja">は</span> is read “wa” and <span lang="ja">へ</span> is read “e”. <span lang="ja">を</span> is read “o”, and today it only appears as a particle.

Learn one row at a time and say each sound out loud. The chart shows the stroke order of every character.

## Dakuten ゛ and handakuten ゜

Two small marks change the consonant. The dakuten <span lang="ja">゛</span>, two short strokes, voices it: k becomes g, s becomes z, t becomes d and h becomes b. So <span lang="ja">か</span> becomes <span lang="ja">が</span> (ga), <span lang="ja">さ</span> becomes <span lang="ja">ざ</span> (za), <span lang="ja">た</span> becomes <span lang="ja">だ</span> (da) and <span lang="ja">は</span> becomes <span lang="ja">ば</span> (ba).

The handakuten <span lang="ja">゜</span>, a small circle, only goes on the h-row and makes a p sound: <span lang="ja">は</span> becomes <span lang="ja">ぱ</span> (pa).

<span lang="ja">じ</span> and <span lang="ja">ぢ</span> both sound “ji”, and <span lang="ja">ず</span> and <span lang="ja">づ</span> both sound “zu”. <span lang="ja">じ</span> and <span lang="ja">ず</span> are the usual spellings; <span lang="ja">ぢ</span> and <span lang="ja">づ</span> appear in a handful of words such as <span lang="ja">はなぢ</span> (hanaji, nosebleed) and <span lang="ja">つづく</span> (tsuzuku, to continue).

## Combinations きゃ・しゅ・ちょ

A character from the i-column followed by a small <span lang="ja">ゃ</span>, <span lang="ja">ゅ</span> or <span lang="ja">ょ</span> makes one syllable, called <span lang="ja"><ruby>拗音<rt>ようおん</rt></ruby></span> (yōon). <span lang="ja">き</span> plus <span lang="ja">ゃ</span> is <span lang="ja">きゃ</span>, read “kya” in a single beat, not “ki-ya”.

Write the small kana at about half size. The size matters: <span lang="ja">きゃく</span> (kyaku, guest) and <span lang="ja">きやく</span> (kiyaku, terms and conditions) are different words.

There are 33 combinations, from <span lang="ja">きゃ</span> to <span lang="ja">ぴょ</span>. Some follow the spelling of their row: <span lang="ja">し</span> gives <span lang="ja">しゃ</span> (sha), <span lang="ja">ち</span> gives <span lang="ja">ちゃ</span> (cha) and <span lang="ja">じ</span> gives <span lang="ja">じゃ</span> (ja).

## Small っ and long vowels

A small <span lang="ja">っ</span> doubles the consonant that follows it, like a short pause before the sound: <span lang="ja">きって</span> (kitte, stamp) and <span lang="ja">きて</span> (kite, come) are different words. In romaji the next consonant is written twice.

A long vowel is written by adding a vowel:

- long a adds <span lang="ja">あ</span>: <span lang="ja">おかあさん</span> (okāsan, mother);
- long i adds <span lang="ja">い</span>: <span lang="ja">おにいさん</span> (onīsan, older brother);
- long u adds <span lang="ja">う</span>: <span lang="ja">くうき</span> (kūki, air);
- long e usually adds <span lang="ja">い</span>, as in <span lang="ja">せんせい</span> (sensei, teacher), and sometimes <span lang="ja">え</span>, as in <span lang="ja">おねえさん</span> (onēsan, older sister);
- long o usually adds <span lang="ja">う</span>, as in <span lang="ja">がっこう</span> (gakkō, school), and sometimes <span lang="ja">お</span>, as in <span lang="ja">おおきい</span> (ōkii, big).

Length changes meaning, so hold the sound: <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> (obasan, aunt) and <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> (obāsan, grandmother) are different people.
