intro:
  - Katakana is the second Japanese syllabary. It has the same sounds as hiragana, character for character, but its shapes are straighter and more angular, so か is カ and さ is サ.
  - Its main job is words from other languages, such as コーヒー (kōhī, coffee) and テレビ (terebi, television). Foreign names and many sound effects in manga are written in katakana too.
  - Writers also use it for emphasis, much like italics in English.
  - If you already know hiragana, you know every katakana sound. What is new is the shapes, the long-vowel mark ー and a few combinations made for foreign sounds.

## Basic 46

The table is the same as in hiragana: vowels across, consonants down, ending with <span lang="ja">ン</span>. Many pairs look related because both scripts grew out of the same kanji: <span lang="ja">カ</span> and <span lang="ja">か</span> both come from <span lang="ja">加</span>, and <span lang="ja">セ</span> and <span lang="ja">せ</span> from <span lang="ja">世</span>. Others look nothing alike, such as <span lang="ja">ア</span> and <span lang="ja">あ</span>.

Some shapes are easy to mix up. In <span lang="ja">シ</span> (shi) the dots sit on the left and the last stroke rises from the bottom; in <span lang="ja">ツ</span> (tsu) the dots sit across the top and the last stroke falls from the top right. <span lang="ja">ン</span> (n) and <span lang="ja">ソ</span> (so) differ in the same way. Practise those pairs side by side.

<span lang="ja">ヲ</span> exists but is rare: in modern writing the particle is written in hiragana, <span lang="ja">を</span>.

## Dakuten and handakuten

The marks work exactly as in hiragana. With the dakuten <span lang="ja">カ</span> becomes <span lang="ja">ガ</span> (ga) and <span lang="ja">ハ</span> becomes <span lang="ja">バ</span> (ba); with the handakuten <span lang="ja">ハ</span> becomes <span lang="ja">パ</span> (pa).

<span lang="ja">ヂ</span> and <span lang="ja">ヅ</span> are as rare as their hiragana twins; <span lang="ja">ジ</span> and <span lang="ja">ズ</span> are used instead.

## Combinations キャ・シュ・チョ

The 33 combinations match hiragana: <span lang="ja">キャ</span> (kya), <span lang="ja">シュ</span> (shu), <span lang="ja">チョ</span> (cho).

Loanwords need sounds that Japanese did not have, so katakana adds combinations with a small vowel: <span lang="ja">ファ</span> (fa) as in <span lang="ja">ファン</span> (fan, fan), <span lang="ja">ティ</span> (ti) as in <span lang="ja">パーティー</span> (pātī, party) and <span lang="ja">ウィ</span> (wi) as in <span lang="ja">ウィンドウ</span> (windō, a window on a screen). You will meet them as you read; they are not part of the basic table.

## Long vowels ー and small ッ

In katakana a long vowel is written with a bar, <span lang="ja">ー</span>, called <span lang="ja"><ruby>長音符<rt>ちょうおんぷ</rt></ruby></span> (chōonpu): <span lang="ja">ケーキ</span> (kēki, cake), <span lang="ja">ノート</span> (nōto, notebook). In vertical text the bar turns vertical too.

A small <span lang="ja">ッ</span> doubles the next consonant, just like <span lang="ja">っ</span> in hiragana: <span lang="ja">ベッド</span> (beddo, bed), <span lang="ja">ネット</span> (netto, internet).

Together they let katakana follow the rhythm of the original word: <span lang="ja">スーパー</span> (sūpā, supermarket) comes from the English “super”.
